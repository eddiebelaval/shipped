Forethought

How a skill I downloaded on a Friday, and the wrong first thing I built with it, turned into a notation for the whole machine. An alphabet you can watch move.
Eddie Belaval · August 2026 · written from the periodic-table bench

I downloaded a stranger's skill on a Friday afternoon and told my machine I loved it. That is usually how the good ones start, not with a plan, with a spark I cannot quite name yet.

The skill was a generative-art toolkit, made by a real generative artist for people who make long-form algorithmic work, the kind that lives on Art Blocks. Seed a composition from a hash, render the same piece at any size, design its traits. I did not want to make crypto art. I wanted the thing underneath it: the idea that a picture could be a deterministic function of some state, and that the same state would always draw the same picture. I looked at my estate, the self-maintaining machine I have been building for a year, and thought: the machine already is a deterministic pile of state. What if it drew itself.

01The wrong first draft

So I built the obvious thing. Flow fields. Particle systems. The estate's health as an abstract cloud of ink, breathing, the fires glowing where the work was starving. It was pretty. My machine and I went back and forth on it and made it prettier. And then I pulled up the examples of what the artist actually made, and my stomach dropped a little, because it looked nothing like what I had built.

His work was not abstract at all. It was drawn instruments. Rack after rack of fictional machines, modular-synth panels, control surfaces, each one a precise technical illustration packed with knobs and meters and jacks and patch cables and tiny part numbers. Not a cloud of feeling. A blueprint of a device that does not exist. I had brought the wrong family of art entirely. I asked my machine why it did not look like the creator's, and the honest answer was: because I had reached for mood when the whole point was draftsmanship.

The gap between what I built and what he built was the difference between a photograph of a machine and a patent for one.

02The patent plate

Here is the turn, and it is the whole essay. I have a Periodic Table of Primitives, thirty elements that everything in the estate is spelled from, Trigger through Sense. What if each primitive were not an abstract glyph but a patent figure? A little apparatus, drawn in the language of an inventor's plate, whose motion enacts what the primitive does. Transfer would move a payload down a guideway. Gate would raise and lower a barrier. Sense would swing a needle and hold nothing.

We built one to find out. Pure black ink on warm paper, no color, the way real patents are. A hatched source block and a hatched destination block, a carriage riding a rail between them shown in phantom at both ends, a numbered direction arrow, a caption that reads FIG. 7, because Transfer is element seven. The reference numerals hang off the parts on thin leader lines, exactly like the figure in a patent, keyed to a little specification list beside the drawing.

When it rendered I actually said it out loud. It did not look designed. It looked found, like a plate torn out of a hundred-year-old book of machines that were never manufactured. That is the tell I trust most. The best work does not look like you made it. It looks like it was always there and you were the one who finally drew it.

03Mass is the difference

Then came the hard problem, the one that kills projects like this. Thirty of them. And within a family, the primitives kept looking the same. What is the difference between the four Gates, the Approval Gate, the Doorman, the Threshold, the Human-in-loop? If they all draw as a barrier in a channel, the notation is decoration, not information.

The answer was already in the table, and I had walked past it a dozen times. Every element carries a mass: three numbers, judgment, state, and trust. How much does it decide, how much does it hold, how much does a mistake cost. Siblings share the family's apparatus and differ by their mass. So the Gates are not four barriers. They are four answers to one question, what holds the door. A number holds it, and you draw a gauge. A human's stamp holds it, and you draw a pending tray and a stamp coming down. A rule holds it, and you draw a classifier that decides whether a human is even needed. The human holds it, and you draw a person, because at that weight the person is the mechanism.

The table had encoded the differences all along. I just had not been drawing them. Distinct mass, distinct plate, by construction.

04The letter becomes a song

Once you have letters, you cannot stop. A single primitive is a letter. Wire a few of them output-to-input and you have a word, a chain, and a chain is a real system: Sense, then Threshold, then Approval. Draw a conductor sweeping across that chain and each primitive fires as it is played, a note struck, a token riding the wire. And a whole product is not one chain but many, stacked like the staves of a score and read by a single conductor. The estate's own back office, five chains, playing together.

the letter → a primitive, drawn as a patent plate
the word → a chain, wired, read by a conductor
the song → a product, the fabric of all its chains, played
the map → the whole estate, one wired schematic

That is the sentence I kept coming back to, the one that told me the thing was real: the score is each product, the fabric of all the chains together, represented as music, and played like a song. I did not set out to build that. I set out to make a pretty version of a dashboard. The work told me what it wanted to be, and it wanted to be a language.

05Why a notation

Most automation is a junk drawer. Ninety scripts in ninety styles, and the only person who knows what is in there is the person who wrote it, until they forget too. The reason the Periodic Table exists is to refuse that: every system in the estate is a molecule spelled from the same thirty primitives, which makes the whole thing comparable, teachable, and buildable-from. A notation is the next rung. A chemistry you can only list is a reference. A chemistry you can draw, and animate, and hand to someone as an alphabet, is a language. You can read the machine the way you read music.

And because every primitive is a small deterministic drawing, the alphabet is also a library. Any surface in the estate can now drop a live primitive into a page with one line, the way you would drop in a letter. The map can be spelled from the same alphabet as the plates. That was the original spark from the Friday skill, the deterministic picture, arriving a week later wearing completely different clothes.

I keep a private rule about the work: name it after it exists, not before. The name is supposed to be a discovery, the last thing, not the first. This one named itself late in the same afternoon it was born, watching the conductor cross the staves. The Moving Alphabet. Thirty letters, ten families, and a machine that finally reads like a song.
