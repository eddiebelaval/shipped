It was a quiet Sunday on the frontier. Then Monday arrived, and two separate moves made the same question explicit.

OpenAI published its internal pretraining perplexity scores for the first time, proposed that frontier labs agree on a shared metric and common limits, and called it "Perplexity and Intelligence." The essay is from Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist. It is not an op-ed. It is a proposal.

Meanwhile, Anthropic's stock, ANT, opened the week at $87 and change. That is below $97.17, the day-one close. That is below the high of $115.10. It is four calendar days since the largest IPO in history. The market is finding its price.

The two things are not unrelated.

Lead Story

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published a research essay on November 22 called "Perplexity and Intelligence." It is the first time OpenAI has shared its internal pretraining metrics publicly, and it arrives paired with a governance proposal: Pachocki argues that frontier labs should adopt pretraining log-perplexity as a shared yardstick and agree on common limits they would not cross until monitoring and safety mechanisms hit concrete milestones.

The mechanism is worth understanding precisely. Log-perplexity measures how "surprised" a model is by text from a held-out distribution. It decreases as you scale compute, data, and architecture. Pachocki published approximate values for OpenAI's internal dataset: around 0.99 for GPT-3.5, 0.91 for GPT-4, and around 0.76 for the model that began producing new mathematical results. His forecast: transformative capabilities he cannot name in advance in a window around 0.68 to 0.70.

The proposal is that labs should not cross that window without first verifying that monitoring tools, interpretability, and safety evaluation frameworks are good enough to understand what they have. It is a voluntary ceiling on the most important variable in AI development, specified in a number.

The blast radius is not technical. No one's training job gets cancelled because Pachocki published a blog post. The blast radius is political. For the first time, a frontier lab chief scientist has proposed a specific, measurable threshold and said: this is the number we should not pass alone. That changes what a governance proposal looks like. It gives regulators a quantity to point at. It gives other labs a thing to agree or disagree with in public, and silence is also a vote.

Pachocki acknowledges the limits of his own metric: perplexity is hard to compare across labs using different datasets and tokenizers. It does not map directly to downstream task performance. It ignores capability gains from post-training, chain-of-thought, and scaffolding. The dataset he used is not public. These are not minor caveats. A speed limit enforced by one lab measuring its own speed is not a neutral constraint. It is a first draft.

The pattern connects to a sequence OpenAI has been building. In October, the company disclosed that GPT-6.2 Astra triggered its internal safety evaluation thresholds. In November, it invited independent safety evaluators into its labs. This paper is the third move: first disclose the internal threshold, then open the labs, then propose the industry adopt the threshold. The sequence is deliberate.

The read: OpenAI is proposing a framework based on a metric it controls. That is worth naming plainly. But the fact that OpenAI is publishing internal training metrics at all is new, and it moves the Overton window toward disclosure. If Anthropic or DeepMind publishes their own perplexity numbers in response, or engages with the specific 0.68 to 0.70 threshold, the conversation becomes a negotiation. If no one responds, it stays interesting and inert.

The contrast is uncomfortable and hard to miss. The same week OpenAI proposed voluntary limits on pretraining compute, Anthropic raised $81.3 billion and listed on Nasdaq to fund more of exactly that. The two labs are pointing in opposite directions in public, in the same week, and the builders deciding which infrastructure to bet on are watching both vectors.

The builder's move: watch the response from the other labs over the next five to seven days. That is the signal. The proposal itself changes nothing about which models you can call today.

Anthropic, Markets, Nov 23
ANT Day 3: The Price the Market Assigned

Anthropic's stock opened Monday at approximately $88, testing territory it has not held since Friday afternoon. Four trading days in, ANT has gone from the $83 IPO price, surged to a high of $115.10 on day one, closed the first day at $97.17, then traced a steady retreat: Friday close near $89, Monday's range between $83 and $92.

The stock is not in freefall. It is still above the IPO price. But it is down more than 20% from its peak, and the narrative that opened the IPO, that the market would sustain a $1.76 trillion valuation on the story of AI as the most important technology in history, is now being written over in real time.

The mechanism is ordinary market behavior in an extraordinary context. IPO buyers at $83 are sitting on a small gain. Anyone who bought in the open market on day one is nursing a loss. The 30-day greenshoe option, which lets underwriters buy 147 million additional shares, may stabilize the floor. At Monday's range, those shares would cost $12 to $13 billion, and the underwriters have no obligation to exercise if the floor is not worth defending.

The pattern: the broader Nasdaq was down 2.2% on Anthropic's IPO day and has stayed soft. The retreat in ANT is happening in a market that is not enthusiastic about tech multiples generally. Anthropic is not being singled out for bad faith. The category is under pressure.

The read: Anthropic is now the only frontier lab with quarterly reporting obligations, revenue disclosure, and margin questions from people who do not have an alignment agenda. That is not a problem the other labs have. It is also not a problem that disappears after the IPO. The safety narrative now has a price-to-earnings expectation attached to it, and the market is not saying the narrative is wrong. The market is saying it needs to reconcile with the growth narrative, and that reconciliation has not happened yet. The interesting year is not 2026. It is 2027, when the first earnings call lands.

The builder's move: nothing operational changes. Claude API pricing and terms are unaffected. Watch the first earnings call.

Anthropic, Claude Code, Nov 22
Claude Code 2.1.330: Enterprise Sign-in and Easel Charts

Claude Code shipped two notable updates in the Nov 21 to Nov 22 window. Version 2.1.330 adds enterprise sign-in through the Admin Console, so organizations managed in Anthropic's Enterprise tier can now enforce centralized enrollment rather than relying on individual API key distribution. The same release includes Easel, a built-in charts mod that surfaces Claude's visualizations directly in the editor. The environment variable CLAUDE_CODE_FULL_SIGN_IN_LINK=1 forces the full sign-in link on enrollment. The --start-dir flag for the self-hosted runner anchors the agent to a specific directory at startup.

Version 2.1.329 adds /tasks to the slash command menu, listing scheduled tasks, /loop wakeups, and running forks in one view, useful for anyone running long-horizon agent loops and wanting a summary of what is queued.

The mechanism on enterprise sign-in is unglamorous and load-bearing. Previously, distributing Claude Code to a large engineering org meant issuing API keys individually. 2.1.330 moves the enrollment step to the admin layer, which is what enterprise security teams require for access reviews and offboarding. The feature is not a headline item. It is the kind of thing that unlocks the enterprise procurement conversation.

The builder's move: claude update to 2.1.330. Configure Admin Console enrollment for org-wide deployments. Use --start-dir /path/to/repo on the self-hosted runner for multi-repo environments.
