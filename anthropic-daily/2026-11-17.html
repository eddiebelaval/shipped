The Open
Setting the scene
Tuesday afternoon, two labs, two theories.

Somewhere in a hotel conference room today, Anthropic’s bankers were taking final orders on the ANT book. Price range: $70 to $78 per share. Implied valuation: around $1 trillion. Expected pricing date: tomorrow, November 18. First trading on Nasdaq: November 19.

Meanwhile, across the country, OpenAI shipped two new models and cut Astra’s API price by 20%.

Both moves carry the same message with opposite handwriting. Anthropic is betting that Claude compounds into the trillion-dollar range, that the market will treat it as infrastructure and price accordingly. OpenAI is betting that cheaper access to frontier-grade intelligence accelerates adoption fast enough to outrun that thesis entirely. Neither camp has proof. Both camps are right about different things. The interesting day is not the IPO; it is what happens to price benchmarks the morning after.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol

The models landed in ChatGPT Work and Codex today for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users, and went live on the API as gpt-6.2-astra and gpt-6.2-sol. The price change is clean: Astra drops from $10 input / $50 output to $8 / $40 per million tokens. No migration required. No model swap. If you are running GPT-6 Astra in production today, your next billing cycle is 20% smaller without touching a line of code.

Sol keeps its prior rates from GPT-6.1 Sol: $2 input, $0.10 cached input, $10 output. At those numbers, Sol is the cheapest path to frontier reasoning in OpenAI’s lineup. On Cognition’s FrontierCode 2.0 benchmark, running both models at maximum reasoning effort, Sol came within 0.5 points of Astra at roughly one-fifth the cost. The implication for builders is direct: for coding workloads that do not specifically require Astra’s ceiling, Sol is now the obvious default. The gap between the two models is real, but it is narrower than the price delta suggests, and most production coding tasks do not need the extra half-point.

The sentence that deserves more attention than it will get is not about price. OpenAI says GPT-6.2 Astra is “the first externally released model to meet the High threshold for AI self-improvement under our Preparedness Framework.” Read that twice. The Preparedness Framework is OpenAI’s internal scoring system for model capability along dimensions that matter for existential risk, and AI self-improvement is one of the specific axes. “High” is the second-highest tier. If the claim is accurate, Astra is now technically categorized by OpenAI as a system capable of contributing meaningfully to the development of its successors. That is either a mature, transparent milestone or a capability disclosure that most readers will scroll past before the paragraph ends. The right response is both: it matters, and it was buried in a product release.

The concurrent restriction makes the picture sharper. OpenAI launched GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol with tighter safeguards specifically for higher-risk cybersecurity use cases. The stated reason: Astra jumps 22 points over GPT-6 Astra on ExploitBench Internal Port. The model got meaningfully better at offensive security tasks, and OpenAI is narrowing access to that specific slice. The fact that the restriction shipped alongside the model, rather than six weeks later, suggests the safety team has the deployment process wired in a way it did not in earlier cycles.

What GPT-6.2 says about the platform overall: OpenAI is on a cadence. This is not a launch; it is a point release. The product team treated it as routine enough to drop mid-Tuesday without a press event. The pricing cut, the Sol benchmark result, and the Preparedness classification all arrived in the same developer community post. The machinery is working. The pace is consistent. The question is whether it is consistent enough to stay ahead of a lab that will be flush with IPO capital in forty-eight hours.

The contrast is not subtle today. Claude Fable 5.2 leads in some external evaluations, and Anthropic is about to test whether the market agrees at a trillion dollars. OpenAI’s answer is Sol at $2 input and Astra at $8 input, both shipping today. These are different theories of winning. Anthropic is competing on perceived frontier quality; OpenAI is competing on cost per token and consistent delivery cadence. Watch which theory compounds faster through Q1 2027.

The builder’s move: check whether your existing Astra workloads are on gpt-6.2-astra or still pointing to a pinned older version. The 20% price drop applies to the new model string automatically. If you want Sol as a cost-optimization swap, the benchmark suggests the tradeoff is favorable for most tasks. Run your own eval on a representative sample before switching production traffic.

Three more moves from the frontier
Google, November 17
Gemini Tasks Opens to Free Users in Six Countries

Google made Gemini Tasks free on November 17 in the United States, Japan, South Korea, Canada, Australia, and India. The feature was previously locked behind AI Pro, which runs $19.99 per month. Free users need a personal Google account and Activity turned on. That is the entire barrier.

Tasks is Google’s 24/7 personal agent inside the Gemini app. It handles things you would otherwise need to be physically present for: booking, calling businesses on your behalf, paying bills, acting on a timer when you are not watching. Free users get the core capability now, though the highest-friction features stay US-first. Calling and texting require a U.S. number and eligible carrier. Payments require a U.S.-issued card. Outside the six countries, Tasks still requires a paid AI Pro or Ultra plan.

The mechanism here is distribution, not capability. Google is not improving Tasks today; it is widening access. Making an agentic product free in six markets simultaneously is how Google turns a feature into infrastructure. Japan, South Korea, and India each have large, tech-forward mobile-first populations where Google is competing against domestic AI assistants and OpenAI’s expanding consumer footprint. The United Kingdom notably did not make the first wave, which is unusual for a Google global rollout and was not explained publicly.

Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has made autonomous task-execution free for personal account users at this scale. Google is running the widest distribution of consumer agentic capabilities of any frontier lab, in six markets simultaneously. The free-to-paid pipeline for agentic features is a different theory of acquisition than OpenAI’s pricing-down-from-the-frontier approach.

OpenAI, November 16
457 Megawatts of AMD Infrastructure, Committed

TensorWave announced it has signed a six-year agreement to supply OpenAI with 457 megawatts of dedicated AI compute capacity on AMD Instinct MI455X GPUs in Helios rack-scale systems. Single-tenant, purpose-built, spread across multiple U.S. data-center sites. First capacity arrives in H1 2027. Full deployment by early 2028.

TensorWave is the first publicly named neocloud under the broader AMD-OpenAI partnership from October 2025, which covers up to 6 gigawatts of AMD GPU capacity in total. The 457 MW represents about 7.6% of that total commitment. TensorWave will need to roughly triple its staff to support the deal.

The strategic read: OpenAI is building AMD-based infrastructure in parallel with its existing Nvidia infrastructure, not as a replacement, but as a hedge. Nvidia’s dominant position in AI compute is showing pressure from cost, geopolitical supply-chain risk, and AMD’s improving software stack. This deal is OpenAI quietly diversifying before it needs to. For comparison, Anthropic’s IPO will give it capital to make similar infrastructure bets; the question is whether Anthropic builds its own compute relationships or remains more dependent on major cloud providers.

Anthropic, November 18 Expected
The IPO That Prices at a Trillion

Anthropic’s IPO roadshow is in its final hours. The company is expected to price ANT shares on the evening of November 18, with first trading on Nasdaq on November 19. The proposed range is $70 to $78 per share on 825 million Class A shares, implying a valuation of roughly $1 trillion at the top of the range, and up to $1.4 trillion on a fully diluted basis.

Anthropic was founded in 2021 by former OpenAI researchers who left over alignment concerns. It has never been a revenue-first company. The valuation being tested tomorrow is a bet that Claude becomes computing infrastructure, not just a product, and that the safety-first positioning is itself a durable competitive advantage in a world where regulators are paying attention. At $1 trillion, Anthropic would be priced above most established tech companies.

The governance structure matters here. The Class A shares being sold have restricted voting rights; the founders and early insiders retain control through supervoting shares. Anthropic, PBC, is a public benefit corporation, which layers a legal obligation to its stated mission alongside the shareholder value obligations that come with going public. How those two obligations interact under earnings pressure is the governance question that will define Anthropic’s next five years as a public company. For builders, nothing about Claude’s pricing or access changes at IPO. What changes is Anthropic’s cost of capital and its reporting obligations.
