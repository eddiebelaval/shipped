OpenAI wants enterprises to trust it so completely that it built a system to prevent itself from looking.

The Open
Six labs. Twenty-four hours.
Trust is not a policy. It is a hardware guarantee.

The week Anthropic went public with a $1.8 trillion market cap and a safety-first pitch, OpenAI sat down with its first enterprise customers and demonstrated something the IPO roadshow couldn't: a cryptographic system that makes it technically impossible for OpenAI to read your prompts while the model processes them. Not a promise. Not a policy. A technical constraint.

That is the shape of the day. Anthropic sold trust as a cultural value. OpenAI is trying to sell it as a hardware guarantee. They are not the same product.

Meanwhile, the great agentic shopping war between Amazon and Meta thaws just enough to suggest a deal is possible. And a small Claude release closes out a week that, for Anthropic, was mostly about the IPO aftermath.

OpenAI, Enterprise

Private
Inference

OpenAI starts testing a system where it cannot see your data. The mechanism is real and verifiable. The timing is pointed.
Lab OpenAI  ·  Area API / Enterprise  ·  Source The Next Web
By the numbers Alpha pilot, select enterprise customers. Part of Private Intelligence: Private Inference, Zero Data Retention, Private Safety Processing. Pricing and model support: not yet disclosed. Performance overhead: not disclosed.
Nov 25, 2026

The word "inference" is usually a technical term. This week, OpenAI made it a promise. In a small alpha pilot with select enterprise customers, the company began testing Private Inference, a system built on confidential computing that processes business prompts on isolated hardware. OpenAI cannot access what runs through it. Not the prompts. Not the responses. The model runs; nothing logs.

The mechanism matters. This is not a data-retention policy that relies on employees following rules. Confidential computing uses hardware-level isolation called Trusted Execution Environments to create an encrypted enclave. The model runs inside. The host, in this case OpenAI, operates outside it. The architectural guarantee is that the host cannot read what the enclave processes, even with root access to the servers. What happens in the enclave stays in the enclave, and that claim can be independently verified through remote attestation, a cryptographic receipt proving the enclave's contents weren't tampered with.

The blast radius matters most to the enterprises that never moved sensitive workloads to any AI platform precisely because they couldn't answer this question: how do we know the vendor isn't training on our data, or that a rogue insider couldn't pull query logs? Private Inference's answer is structural. The data protection is not a legal commitment you're trusting a vendor to honor; it's a system property you can verify.

Sam Altman confirmed the pilot on X. OpenAI hasn't disclosed which models support it, what the performance overhead is, or what the pricing looks like. That last question determines whether this feature matters at scale or stays a showcase. Confidential computing carries meaningful compute overhead, and if the premium over standard inference is large enough, most enterprises will keep their sensitive workloads off-platform rather than pay it.

The read: OpenAI just made a wager that the future of enterprise AI isn't about which lab has the best model. It's about which lab can prove it doesn't look. That's not a safety claim. It's a sales pitch dressed in cryptography, and it lands the week Anthropic's stock dipped below its $83 IPO price on the fourth trading day. Anthropic sold trust as a character trait. OpenAI is selling it as a technical spec.

The contrast is blunt. Anthropic's entire IPO narrative rested on being the lab that takes safety seriously, that earns trust through culture and careful deployment. OpenAI is now offering enterprises something culture can't match: a verifiable guarantee that their data never touches OpenAI's logs at all. The two offerings are aimed at the same CIO who's hesitating before moving sensitive workloads to frontier AI. They are very different answers to that hesitation.

The builder's move: sign up for the Private Inference interest form at openai.com and get on the waitlist. If your firm has been holding back sensitive workloads because of data concerns, this alpha is worth watching closely. When pricing and model support drop, re-evaluate.

Four more moves across the frontier
OpenAI, ChatGPT
OpenAI Merges Chat and Work

OpenAI rolled out a combined Chat and Work experience to a small group of Pro subscribers this week, with the full migration coming in December. The practical change: Plus, Pro, and Business plan users will draw from one usage allowance across ChatGPT and Codex. Right now the two run separate meters. Starting December, one pool, shared.

The blast radius is specific. Heavy Pro users who run both regular ChatGPT and Codex intensively in the same billing window will hit limits sooner. OpenAI says most users won't notice; heavy users will, especially if they've been treating the two meters as separate daily allowances. Instant Chat stays exempt from the shared cap. This is the third usage-limit restructure OpenAI has done in 2026. The direction is consolidation: one bill, one pool, one account. The right move for simplifying enterprise procurement, with predictable friction for users who've been double-counting their access.

Source: BleepingComputer
Meta AI, Amazon
Amazon and Meta Are Talking About Muse

TechCrunch reported Wednesday that Amazon and Meta have held multiple rounds of negotiations to end the two-month blockade on Muse, Meta's AI shopping agent, from Amazon.com. The blockade started September 20 when Amazon cut off Muse for not identifying itself as a bot and for accessing customer account data while acting on user instructions.

Two issues remain unresolved: how search results display inside Muse and how ad revenue flows when a purchase happens through the agent rather than through Amazon's native interface. Both are control questions. Amazon does not want Meta deciding what surfaces in a search query on its own platform. Meta's position is that Muse doesn't see passwords or payment info and that credentials stay in secure storage.

The contrast tells the actual story. Walmart rolled out the welcome mat for Muse on September 21, one day after Amazon blocked it. This is not an agentic shopping problem. It's a specific fight about who controls the transaction layer when AI agents start replacing native apps as the shopping surface. Amazon chose to fight. Walmart decided the agentic traffic was worth the terms. The resolution terms will set a precedent for every agentic commerce deal that follows.

Source: TechCrunch
Anthropic, Claude Code
Monthly Recaps Go GA, MCP Servers Ship Skills

Two smaller Anthropic releases closed the week. Monthly Recaps left beta on November 24, now GA across Team and Enterprise plans. The November update adds Claude Code sessions and Cowork tasks to the memory the Recap draws from, so the monthly digest can now reflect your coding work and collaborative tasks alongside regular Claude conversations. Users can delete stored recaps.

Claude Code shipped v2.1.332 around the same time. The main structural change: MCP servers can now package and ship their own skills directly. A server that previously had to document its capabilities in a README and hope users found them can now ship a .claude/skills/ folder that installs automatically when Claude Code connects. Bug fixes for session freezes when multiple skill files change concurrently are also included. MCP skill shipping is infrastructure, not a headline. But it is the kind of infrastructure that makes the platform stickier as the ecosystem of third-party MCP servers grows.

Source: Claude Code Changelog  ·  Claude Apps Release Notes
Anthropic, Europe
Anthropic Opens Larger Zurich Office

Bloomberg reported Wednesday that Anthropic is moving into a larger office in Zurich, taking about 2,500 square meters at the Fraumünsterpost building in the city center. This is a physical expansion of the existing research-focused Zurich office led by Neil Houlsby since it opened. The move comes one week after Anthropic's IPO closed, suggesting the capital raise is already translating into real-estate decisions.

Post-IPO, Anthropic is investing in research infrastructure. OpenAI is investing in trust infrastructure. Both are forms of moat-building. They are building very different moats.

Source: Bloomberg
Looking ahead
Quiet on the Wire

Google DeepMind was quiet this week. The last release from Mountain View was Gemini 4 Neon on November 18, a frontier coding agent with a 1-million-token context window. No new Gemini release has landed in eight days. That gap is unusual given the pace of November.

Mistral closed a fresh 100 million euro tranche from Deutsche Telekom's T.Capital Tech Fund earlier this week, adding to the 3 billion euro Series D announced in September. The investment is framed as a first step toward a German sovereign-AI partnership. No model or product launch accompanied it.

Anthropic at AWS re:Invent 2026 on November 30. A session on Claude Code agentic workflows is on the schedule. Expect demo material and possibly SDK announcements timed to that stage.

The Close
OpenAI is testing encryption. Amazon is fighting for the checkout layer. Anthropic is leasing office space in Zurich.
The frontier looks different depending on which lab you're watching. Zoom out and the shape is the same: every lab is now competing on trust, on who owns the transaction, on who gets to be the runtime that enterprises put their data into.
That is not a model-capability race. It is an enterprise sales race with AI as the product.
