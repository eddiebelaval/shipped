Sunday, November 15, 2026
A quiet Sunday, the kind the frontier uses to drop something it has been holding.

Anthropic’s IPO roadshow is in its fifth day. Pricing is expected Tuesday. Trading starts Wednesday. The price range is $70 to $78 a share on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. At the high end, the offering values the lab at roughly $65 billion. The bankers are working the phones. The engineers, apparently, are writing physics.

OpenAI published a blog post today crediting GPT-6 Astra with composing the detailed proof work for a candidate lattice definition of the Standard Model. Nathaniel Craig, a particle physicist at UC Santa Barbara and a visiting scientist at OpenAI, says he had a strategy for the construction but could not close the proof for every required case. Astra did. Craig published the result as a preprint. Some sections of the manuscript are Astra-composed proofs, not human-written text that Astra checked.

The week that led here was not quiet. Google DeepMind ran 3 million lines of Lean proofs through Signal’s cryptographic codebase, formally verifying that Signal’s encryption does what Signal claims. That is a verification move, checking something already believed true. Craig and Astra moved the other way: they proposed something new and tried to prove it. Both are using formal proof infrastructure. Both ran it in a different direction. That contrast is the story this weekend.

The Lead

Nathaniel Craig published a blog post on November 15 on what OpenAI is calling its science blog, the lab’s first guest post in what appears to be a new publication channel for external researchers working with OpenAI models. Craig is a particle physicist. His paper, coauthored with GPT-6 Astra, proposes a construction for defining the Standard Model on a four-dimensional spacetime lattice.

The Standard Model is the physicist’s most reliable accounting of how fundamental particles behave under three of the four known forces. It works, famously and precisely, in continuous spacetime. The problem Craig was pursuing is how to put it on a discrete lattice, the kind of four-dimensional grid that underpins numerical simulations in quantum chromodynamics. The specific difficulty is chiral fermions. The weak force treats left-handed and right-handed particles differently. On a lattice, that asymmetry is hard to preserve without introducing mathematical inconsistencies. This is the Nielsen-Ninomiya problem. It has been an open question since at least the late 1990s. Theorists have proposed partial constructions. None closed the full proof.

Craig says he could sketch a strategy for a construction that might work. What he could not do was prove the construction held for every case the proof required. He gave the problem to Astra. Craig’s description of what happened next is specific: Astra wrote the detailed proofs. Sections of the resulting preprint are Astra-composed arguments, incorporated into the paper as proof text. OpenAI’s post says this work builds on the lab’s earlier Yang-Mills existence proof, a formal result establishing that the Yang-Mills quantum field theory exists, as a building block for the new lattice construction.

The result is a candidate, not a solution. Craig says so explicitly. Physical consistency tests for the full coupling structure of the weak force on the lattice remain open. Lean was used to check parts of the proof, but the entire construction has not been formally verified end to end. Some outlets have described the work as “solving” the problem. Craig’s framing is more careful: a step toward, with important open questions.

What is not in dispute: an AI model composed novel mathematical arguments that a credentialed physicist, capable of evaluating them, found convincing enough to publish. That transition from tool to contributor is not theoretical anymore. It happened on a Sunday in November 2026. The question is what category of original mathematical work is next, and how fast the category expands.

The blast radius for builders is not in particle physics, at least not yet. It is in what the existence of this work says about where research AI is operating. Astra did not assist a human in writing a paper. Astra composed novel mathematical arguments that a credentialed physicist found sufficient to publish. The gap between “AI helped me search” and “AI wrote the original argument” just got demonstrably shorter. If Astra can close a 25-year-old physics proof, the question is not whether AI can do original mathematics. The question is what category is next.

The contrast against DeepMind is worth holding at the same time. AlphaProof Code ran 3 million lines of Lean proofs across Signal’s codebase and proved the cryptographic guarantees correct. That is an auditor’s move. It starts from a system known to work and proves it formally. Craig and Astra started from an open conjecture and tried to prove it true. Both require AI that can reason in formal proof languages at scale. The direction of reasoning is opposite. One checks. One proposes. Both converge on Lean as the working infrastructure, and that convergence will matter as the category expands.

OpenAI chose to publish this on a Sunday, the quiet day in the middle of Anthropic’s IPO roadshow week. That is not a scheduling accident. The physics result has been in development. The science blog is new. Dropping both together on the weekend before Anthropic prices draws a comparison without saying one. OpenAI is demonstrating work and letting the contrast speak. That is a colder and more effective move than a benchmark.

Anthropic IPO Roadshow, Day Five: Pricing Tuesday

For anyone watching the valuation math: at $78 per share, the company prices at roughly $65 billion. That number was considered aspirational when the S-1 was filed. It is now the floor of the institutional range.

Anthropic / Claude Code / Nov 13, 2026
Claude Code v2.1.325: Shell Fixes, Opus 4.8 Clock Running

The latest Claude Code version as of November 13 is v2.1.325. Two correctness fixes shipped: Code Mode scripts were ignoring worktrees entered with EnterWorktree, and shell commands were being left running on macOS and Linux when Claude Code exits on its own. Neither is a feature addition. Both are the kind of fix that matters when you are running Claude Code in a CI environment or in a persistent shell session.

The deprecation clock has real stakes attached. Opus 4.8 fast mode was deprecated October 30 and removes November 30. The suggested replacement is Opus 5.5. If you have Claude Code configurations, scripts, or tooling that pins fast mode on Opus 4.8, the November 30 cutoff is not advisory. After that date, calls to that mode return errors. Run claude update to get to v2.1.325, then audit anything that pins Opus 4.8 fast mode.

Source: github.com/anthropics/claude-code CHANGELOG.md
