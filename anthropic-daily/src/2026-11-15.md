## The Lead Story
# The Conjecture and the Proof

**GPT-6 Astra composed original mathematical arguments toward a problem in particle physics that has been open for more than 25 years. The result is a preprint, not a theorem. But the way it happened changes what we can say AI is doing in science.**

Nathaniel Craig published a blog post on November 15 on what OpenAI is calling its science blog, the lab's first guest post in what appears to be a new publication channel for external researchers working with OpenAI models. Craig is a particle physicist. His paper, coauthored with GPT-6 Astra, proposes a construction for defining the Standard Model on a four-dimensional spacetime lattice.

The Standard Model is the physicist's most reliable accounting of how fundamental particles behave under three of the four known forces. It works, famously and precisely, in continuous spacetime. The problem Craig was pursuing is how to put it on a discrete lattice, the kind of four-dimensional grid that underpins numerical simulations in quantum chromodynamics. The specific difficulty is chiral fermions. The weak force treats left-handed and right-handed particles differently. On a lattice, that asymmetry is hard to preserve without introducing mathematical inconsistencies. This is the Nielsen-Ninomiya problem. It has been an open question since at least the late 1990s. Theorists have proposed partial constructions. None closed the full proof.

Craig says he could sketch a strategy for a construction that might work. What he could not do was prove the construction held for every case the proof required. He gave the problem to Astra. Craig's description of what happened next is specific: Astra wrote the detailed proofs. Sections of the resulting preprint are Astra-composed arguments, incorporated into the paper as proof text. OpenAI's post says this work builds on the lab's earlier Yang-Mills existence proof, a formal result establishing that the Yang-Mills quantum field theory exists, as a building block for the new lattice construction.

The result is a candidate, not a solution. Craig says so explicitly. Physical consistency tests for the full coupling structure of the weak force on the lattice remain open. Lean was used to check parts of the proof, but the entire construction has not been formally verified end to end. Some outlets have described the work as "solving" the problem. Craig's framing is more careful: a step toward, with important open questions.

The blast radius for builders is not in particle physics, at least not yet. It is in what the existence of this work says about where research AI is operating. Astra did not assist a human in writing a paper. Astra composed novel mathematical arguments that a credentialed physicist, capable of evaluating them, found convincing enough to publish. That transition from tool to contributor is not theoretical anymore. It happened on a Sunday in November 2026. The question is what category of original mathematical work is next, and how fast the category expands.

The contrast against DeepMind is worth holding in parallel. AlphaProof Code ran 3 million lines of Lean proofs across Signal's codebase and proved the cryptographic guarantees correct. That is an auditor's move. It starts from a system known to work and proves it formally. Impressive, and the blast radius for security-critical software is wide. Craig and Astra started from an open conjecture and tried to prove it true. The capabilities involved are related, both require AI that can reason in formal proof languages at scale, but the direction of reasoning is opposite. One checks. One proposes.

What both moments share is Lean. The formal proof language that mathematicians spent decades building as a niche academic tool is now the working infrastructure for AI-era mathematical verification. AlphaProof Code generates Lean proofs. The Craig-Astra paper uses Lean for verification. OpenAI's Yang-Mills result was formalized in Lean. DeepMind's AlphaProof line runs in Lean. The frontier converged on a tool, and that convergence will matter as the category of AI-assisted mathematics expands.

The read: OpenAI chose to publish this on a Sunday, the quiet day in the middle of Anthropic's IPO roadshow week. That is not a scheduling accident. The physics result has been in development. The science blog is new. Dropping both together on the weekend before Anthropic prices draws a comparison without saying one. OpenAI is not saying it is ahead. It is demonstrating work and letting the contrast speak. That is a colder and more effective move than a benchmark.

### Claude Code v2.1.325, Shell Fixes
The latest Claude Code version as of November 13 is v2.1.325. Two correctness fixes shipped: Code Mode scripts were ignoring worktrees entered with EnterWorktree, and shell commands were being left running on macOS and Linux when Claude Code exits on its own. Neither is a feature addition. Both are the kind of fix that matters when you are running Claude Code in a CI environment or in a persistent shell session.

The deprecation clock has real stakes attached: Opus 4.8 fast mode was deprecated October 30 and removes November 30. The suggested replacement is Opus 5.5. If you have Claude Code configurations, scripts, or tooling that pins fast mode on Opus 4.8, the November 30 cutoff is not advisory. After that date, calls to that mode return errors.

**Builder's move:** Run `claude update` to get to v2.1.325. Audit anything that pins Opus 4.8 fast mode and migrate to Opus 5.5 before November 30.
